Title

Good morning, and thank you all for being here. My name is Assad Abid, and today I will present my MS Artificial Intelligence thesis, titled Biomass Calculation and Carbon Stock Estimation Using Satellite Imagery. The work was carried out at SEECS, NUST, supervised by Dr. Faisal Shafait and co-supervised by Dr. Momina Moetesum. I also thank my committee members, Dr. Junaid Younas and Dr. M Ali Tahir. The core idea is to estimate forest biomass using only standard RGB imagery, with no specialised sensors.

Slide 02 / 28

Why Forest Biomass, and Why RGB-only Is Hard

Let me start with why this matters. Forests are the largest terrestrial carbon store, holding around 861 petagrams of carbon and absorbing a few petagrams more each year. The quantity we care about is above-ground biomass, or AGB, because it is the most directly observable part of forest carbon and the variable used in IPCC inventories, REDD+ reporting, and carbon credits. The problem is that global biomass maps still disagree by twenty to fifty percent in difficult regions. The most accurate sensors, like LiDAR, are expensive and cover only samples, while multispectral and radar saturate at high biomass. In countries like Pakistan, LiDAR is rarely available and inventories are small. So the driving question is: can we estimate biomass accurately using only the most widely available imagery, ordinary high-resolution RGB?

Slide 03 / 28

Hand-Crafted & Sensor-Based AGB Estimation

The literature splits into two parts. The first is classical, hand-crafted and sensor-based estimation. Review and field studies, such as Zhu, Liang, and Liu, extract texture features from the gray-level co-occurrence matrix, vegetation indices, and band statistics, and feed them to machine-learning regressors. These work, but the engineered features transfer poorly between sites. At the other end, LiDAR studies like Duncanson give the strongest structural signal, but coverage is sparse and acquisition is costly. The common limitation is that hand-crafted descriptors capture only low-order statistics, and LiDAR is simply unavailable across most of the world. These hand-crafted features become the 97-feature baselines I benchmark against later.

Slide 04 / 28

Learned Representations & the Closest Prior Work

The second part is learned representations. Self-supervised vision models, especially the DINO family from Caron and colleagues, produce transferable, domain-stable features with no fine-tuning, and this is the backbone of my work. In forestry, Tolan and Wan recover canopy height from RGB, but they depend on LiDAR labels, and Hao regresses biomass directly from self-supervised features, but only on a single controlled crop. So the research gap is clear: no study uses frozen, RGB-only self-supervised descriptors for plot-level forest biomass across distinct ecosystems, without fine-tuning, LiDAR, or multispectral data. My contribution is exactly that pipeline, validated at two contrasting sites.

Slide 05 / 28

Inside the Hand-Crafted Baselines: GLCM & Indices

Before moving on, I want to detail the hand-crafted baselines, because they are what I compare against. The RGB-only literature uses three fixed feature families. First, GLCM texture from Haralick: six features computed over the gray-level co-occurrence matrix, contrast, dissimilarity, homogeneity, angular second moment, energy, and correlation, taken at multiple pixel offsets and orientations. Second, five visible-band vegetation indices, ExG, GLI, VARI, RGBVI, and MGRVI, which approximate the chlorophyll signal from the red, green, and blue channels. Third, per-channel band statistics: the mean, standard deviation, skewness, and kurtosis of each channel. Together these concatenate into a 97-dimensional vector, and I show that frozen DINO descriptors beat this baseline by about 21 percent at Balakot and 24 percent at Karlsruhe.

Slide 06 / 28

Gaps Identified in the Literature

Pulling the literature together, I identify five gaps. One, modality cost: policy-grade accuracy usually needs expensive LiDAR or multispectral sensors. Two, a feature ceiling: hand-crafted RGB features capture only low-order statistics. Three, untested transfer: frozen self-supervised descriptors are proven for canopy height and crops, but not for plot-level forest biomass. Four, no cross-ecosystem evidence: no one tests a single RGB-only pipeline across distinct forests. And five, small samples: inventories have fewer than 150 plots, so high-dimensional features overfit. This thesis sits in the unfilled space, at the intersection of the most accessible sensor and the most modern features.

Slide 07 / 28

The Problem in One Statement

This leads to the problem statement. There is no accurate, data-efficient, and operationally affordable framework for plot-level forest biomass estimation and wall-to-wall carbon mapping that relies only on RGB imagery, requires no site-specific training of a deep feature extractor, and stays robust at the small sample sizes, fewer than 150 plots, typical of national inventories in developing regions. Three constraints define the work: RGB-only, frozen, and small-sample robust.

Slide 08 / 28

One Central Question, Five Sub-Questions

From the problem I derive one central question and five sub-questions. The central question: can a frozen self-supervised ViT, used as a fixed extractor on RGB imagery, support accurate plot-level biomass regression across two distinct ecosystems, without site-specific fine-tuning? The sub-questions ask about feature transfer, which lightweight regressor works best at small n, whether leakage-free correlation selection beats the full vector, whether unsupervised clustering helps heterogeneous landscapes, and whether the unchanged pipeline gives consistent gains at both sites.

Slide 09 / 28

Four Interrelated Contributions

The objectives map to four contributions. C1, build the RGB-only pipeline on a frozen DINO with lightweight regressors. C2, show that correlation-based descriptor selection is an effective, interpretable regulariser. C3, propose optional unsupervised clustering with cluster-specific models for heterogeneous landscapes. And C4, validate against hand-crafted baselines at two sites and generate wall-to-wall carbon rasters.

Slide 10 / 28

A Five-Stage RGB-Only Pipeline

Here is the methodology, a five-stage pipeline. First, I extract a plot-centred RGB patch, resize to 224 by 224, and normalise with ImageNet statistics. Second, I pass it through a frozen DINO ViT-Small to get a 384-dimensional CLS-token descriptor, with no fine-tuning. Third, I rank the dimensions by their absolute Pearson correlation with biomass on the training set only, and keep the top N. Fourth, I embed with UMAP and cluster with K-means, fitting cluster-specific models where the data support it. Fifth, I regress with NNLS, ridge, or XGBoost. The key principle is that the encoder is frozen, so the same backbone serves both sites and only the small regressor is fitted per site.

Slide 11 / 28

Non-Negative Least Squares (NNLS)

My primary regressor is non-negative least squares, and I want to explain it carefully. NNLS is instance-based. For a query patch, I find its k nearest training plots in descriptor space and reconstruct the query descriptor as a non-negative combination of those neighbours, subject to the weights summing to one. That is the first equation: minimise the squared error between A times w and b, where A holds the neighbour descriptors and b is the query, subject to w non-negative and summing to one. Then I predict biomass as the same weighted sum of the neighbours' AGB values. The simplex constraint is the important part: because the prediction is a convex combination of training labels, it can never extrapolate beyond the observed range or return a negative biomass. It is solved with the classical active-set algorithm, and its only hyperparameters are the neighbourhood size and the distance metric, chosen by cross-validation. This is why it is robust at small n, physically grounded, and interpretable, and it gives the lowest error at both sites.

Slide 12 / 28

Two Geographically Distinct Study Areas

For data, I use two geographically distinct study areas. Balakot in northern Pakistan is subtropical to temperate montane forest, with 25 plots, 16 forested and 9 zero-biomass, ranging up to about 26 tons per hectare, at 0.23 metre resolution, validated with nested leave-one-out. Karlsruhe in Germany is managed temperate lowland forest, with 113 plots, ranging up to about 302 tons per hectare, at 0.177 metre resolution, validated with an 85/15 split. The same recipe runs on both, spanning zero to 302 tons per hectare across two forest types and contrasting image scales, which makes this a demanding cross-ecosystem test.

Slide 13 / 28

How Biomass Is Distributed, and Why It Matters

The biomass distributions explain two design choices. In Balakot the distribution is bimodal: a zero-biomass mode from nine non-forested plots and a forested mode. In Karlsruhe the forest plots span 100 to 300 tons per hectare across six species, plus 12 non-forest patches set to zero, and the descriptor space forms clear clusters where feature distance no longer tracks biomass difference, so a single global model struggles. These motivate zero-biomass anchors for non-forest, and unsupervised clustering with per-cluster regressors for Germany.

Slide 14 / 28

Why These Design Choices

Each design choice has a clear justification. Frozen DINO gives embeddings stable across domains, so a new site needs only a small regressor. The correlation filter is closed-form and interpretable and nests leakage-free inside the cross-validation folds. NNLS keeps predictions within the observed range. UMAP plus K-means separates structurally distinct stands and falls back to a single model when only one group is supported. And zero-biomass anchors suppress artefacts in the maps at no extra data cost. Every choice minimises cost and labelled-data demand while staying robust at small n.

Slide 15 / 28

Alternatives Considered & Benchmarked

I also considered and benchmarked alternatives. The hand-crafted 97-dimensional feature stack was re-implemented as six literature baselines, tuned with the same effort, and beaten at both sites. Fine-tuning the encoder needs large labelled data and defeats the low-cost goal. LiDAR or multispectral fusion was excluded by design because it is unavailable in Pakistan. XGBoost overfits badly at 25 plots, so it is kept only for comparison. And the full 384-dimensional vector underperforms a ranked subset. A controlled comparison is telling: hand-crafted XGBoost and DINO XGBoost both overfit badly at n equals 25, which isolates the feature source and shows the gain is not the regressor alone but the pairing of the descriptors with NNLS.

Slide 16 / 28

Implementation & Validation Protocol

On implementation and validation: patches differ slightly per site to match plot geometry, and all are ImageNet-normalised. Pakistan uses nested leave-one-out with descriptor ranking inside each fold to prevent leakage. Germany uses an 85/15 split within clusters with 5-fold inner cross-validation. I sweep the descriptor count from 20 to 300, and report percentage RMSE as the primary metric, plus RMSE, MAE, and R squared. Everything is seeded for reproducibility, and because the encoder is frozen, a full run finishes in minutes on a single mid-range GPU.

Slide 17 / 28

Balakot: DINO + NNLS Leads

Now the results, starting with Pakistan. DINO with NNLS leads at a percentage RMSE of 24.71 and R squared of 0.922, ahead of the hand-crafted random forest baseline at 31.12 and of ridge at 31.73. XGBoost is worst, overfitting at 25 plots. That is about 21 percent lower relative error than the best hand-crafted baseline, and every selection decision was made on training data only. The scatter plots confirm it: NNLS clusters tightly on the one-to-one line with no high-biomass saturation, while XGBoost disperses. Forcing all 384 descriptors, with the estimator held fixed so only selection is ablated, degrades NNLS to 33.56 — an 8.85 point gap that is attributable to descriptor selection alone.

Slide 18 / 28

The Descriptors Carry Real Biomass Signal

I also checked that the descriptors carry real biomass signal, not just curve-fitting. Across the 384 dimensions, correlations with biomass span from about minus 0.84 to plus 0.87, and 171 of them, about 44 and a half percent, are significant at p below 0.05. The top descriptor reaches an r of 0.871, and the top ten all exceed 0.814 in magnitude. The close agreement between Pearson and Spearman confirms approximately linear relationships, which justifies correlation ranking. The signal is spread broadly, but the highest-correlation dimensions concentrate it through a simple, interpretable filter.

Slide 19 / 28

Karlsruhe: Robust Generalisation

Germany tests generalisation on a larger, more heterogeneous dataset. Again DINO with NNLS leads, at a percentage RMSE of 17.40 and R squared of 0.837 on the eighty-five fifteen split, which is about 24 percent lower relative error than the best baseline, ridge on hand-crafted features at 22.79. Because seventeen test plots is a small sample, I repeated the whole pipeline over twenty stratified splits: DINO with NNLS averages 22.99 plus or minus 3.65 and still leads the best baseline at 24.43. The hand-crafted stacking ensemble collapses to a negative R squared, overfitting at this sample size. The held-out scatter, coloured by cluster, shows tight agreement.

Slide 20 / 28

Clustering, Sensitivity & Ablations

This slide covers clustering, sensitivity, and ablations. The UMAP embedding shows Germany splitting into two coherent clusters, dense high-biomass and wide-range. Pakistan appears to show two blobs as well, but that is a projection artefact, as I will come to; the two German clusters share 23 of their top 70 descriptors, so two thirds differ. The sensitivity curve is now the train-only inner cross-validation error against K, per cluster, which is where K is actually chosen; the NNLS curve is flat to within about one point over a wide range. The ablations are conditional rather than universal: both selection and clustering help NNLS but hurt ridge and XGBoost. A size-matched control separates the two things clustering does, because it specialises each model but also cuts its training set from 96 rows to 37 and 59. For NNLS the specialisation is worth 4.2 points and more than repays the 1.2 points of lost data, so clustering genuinely helps the reconstruction regressor. For XGBoost almost all the damage is data starvation, 4.4 of 5.1 points, so the fair statement is that XGBoost needs data, not that structure hurts it. And at Balakot forcing clustering is catastrophic, taking NNLS from 24.71 to 64.62, because that descriptor space has no real cluster structure at all: silhouette 0.14 in the raw feature space against 0.37 for Karlsruhe. The two blobs you see in the UMAP plot are an artefact of the projection, not the data.

Slide 21 / 28

Is It the Encoder, or Just Deep Features?

This slide answers the obvious challenge: is the gain really about self-supervision, or would any big pretrained network do? To test that, I reran the entire pipeline unchanged and swapped only the encoder, using a frozen ImageNet-supervised ResNet50 with 2048 dimensions instead of the 384-dimensional DINO descriptor. The supervised CNN is worse at both sites, and at neither site does it beat the hand-crafted baselines. More telling is that the ranking of regressors inverts: NNLS is the best regressor on DINO descriptors at both sites, 24.71 and 17.40, and the worst on ResNet50, 60.33 and 31.97, while the parametric models are far less affected — on the CNN features XGBoost is the best regressor at Balakot and ridge at Karlsruhe. Since NNLS predicts purely from cosine distances between descriptors, this only makes sense if the self-supervised space has a meaningful metric and the supervised one does not. So the contribution is the geometry of the embedding, not depth and not dimensionality.

Slide 22 / 28

From Plots to Carbon Maps

Finally, I turn plot-level predictions into wall-to-wall carbon maps. In Pakistan, NNLS and ridge produce smooth, spatially coherent gradients, while XGBoost is noisier, and spatial regularity matters for area-integrated stock reporting. In Germany, the zero-biomass anchors correctly drive non-forest areas, like roads and fields, toward near-zero biomass, which reduces artefacts across the 1,030-hectare scene. Converting biomass to carbon is then a fixed scalar: the IPCC default carbon fraction of 0.47, and carbon dioxide equivalent as carbon times 44 over 12. Integrating the NNLS rasters gives about 6.05 tons of carbon per hectare over the 76 hectares mapped at Balakot, roughly 459 tons of carbon or 1,685 tons of CO2 equivalent; and about 84 tons of carbon per hectare over the 1,030 hectares at Karlsruhe, roughly 86,500 tons of carbon or 317,000 tons of CO2 equivalent. Because the conversion is a fixed scalar, all of the map uncertainty is inherited from the biomass prediction. This is the practical output, a low-cost carbon map from RGB alone.

Slide 23 / 28

How the Questions Are Answered & Aims Met

To synthesise, every research question is answered. Q1, yes, frozen descriptors recover variance up to an R squared of 0.922. Q2, NNLS wins on both sites. Q3, correlation-ranked selection beats the full vector for NNLS, though not for the other regressors. Q4, clustering improves NNLS in Germany but not the parametric models, and it fails outright at 25 plots. And Q5, the unchanged pipeline gives about 21 and 24 percent lower error at the two sites. All four contributions are met: the pipeline is built, selection is quantified, clustering is proposed and ablated, and the method is benchmarked with carbon rasters at two sites.

Slide 24 / 28

What These Numbers Do Not Show

Before concluding, I want to be explicit about what these numbers do not show. First, the R squared values are flattered by the zero-biomass anchors, because those anchors widen the label variance that R squared is measured against. If I score only the forested plots, R squared falls from 0.922 to 0.817 at Balakot and from 0.837 to 0.427 at Karlsruhe. Interestingly the percentage RMSE actually improves on forest-only, to 16.05 and 16.34, because the forested mean is higher — so the model is not weaker inside the forest, but R squared has to be read with the anchors in mind. Second, at Balakot five of the sixteen forested plots carry the identical label, 25.77 tons per hectare, which is the ceiling of the allometric model, so part of the fit is reproducing a saturated reference rather than resolving real variation. Third, the test sets are small — seventeen held-out plots in Germany and twenty-five under leave-one-out in Pakistan — which is why I report the twenty-split spread of 22.99 plus or minus 3.65 alongside the headline. And fourth, I have one image acquisition per site, so there is no seasonal validation and no leave-one-site-out test; only the frozen encoder is shared across sites, not the regressor. I would rather state these plainly than have them found for me.

Slide 25 / 28

Conclusions & Future Work

In conclusion: self-supervised features suffice, a frozen DINO encodes enough canopy structure for accurate RGB-only biomass with no LiDAR or fine-tuning. Selection and clustering are conditional rather than universal: both serve the reconstruction regressor and mildly hurt the parametric ones. Critically, swapping the self-supervised encoder for an ImageNet-supervised ResNet50, with everything else identical, is worse at both sites despite five times the dimensionality, and it inverts the ranking of regressors. That tells us the advantage is the geometry of the embedding, not pretrained depth. And operationally, high-resolution carbon mapping is now feasible from standard aerial RGB and a public vision model, lowering the cost barrier for data-sparse regions. Future work includes leave-one-site-out transfer, larger multi-biome inventories, temporal stability, systematic zero-biomass sampling, and larger backbones.
