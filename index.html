Motivation

Why Automate

  • The stock matters. IPCC accounting, REDD+ and carbon credits report it.
  • Monitoring needs 10 to 20% error, refreshed annually.
  • Survey cannot reach that cadence. Where need is greatest, LiDAR is unavailable and inventories run to 100 plots.

Method · Primary Regressor

Non-Negative Least Squares

An instance-based regressor. It reconstructs a query from its k nearest training plots, then applies those weights to their measured AGB.

Reconstruct the query under a simplex constraint
minw ‖ A w − b ‖₂²  subject to  w ≥ 0,  1ᵀw = 1
Predict from the neighbour labels
ŷ = Σi=1k wᵢ yᵢ

A holds the neighbour descriptors, b the query, y their AGB. k and the metric are cross-validated inside each training fold.

Why NNLS here

  • Small-sample robust. No global parametric fit.
  • Bounded. A convex combination cannot go negative.
  • Interpretable. A mixture over named plots.

The same property is a ceiling

Inside the convex hull, NNLS cannot predict above the densest surveyed plot.

Lawson & Hanson (1974); Bro & De Jong (1997) NUST · SEECS

Limitations

What These Numbers Do Not Show

  • The label distribution flatters the metric. Zero-biomass plots dominate the variance, so R² largely rewards separating forest from non-forest. Within forest it falls to 0.427 at Karlsruhe.
  • Appearance only goes so far. RGB records the canopy surface, not the height carrying the biomass. The largest error, 65 t ha⁻¹, comes from a plot whose retrieved neighbours look almost identical to it.
  • Generalisation is indicated, not established. Two sites, one acquisition each, with the regressor refitted at both. The Karlsruhe headline rests on 17 test plots, split randomly rather than spatially.

Appendix A2 · Rationale

Why These Design Choices

  • Frozen DINO.Ablated Stable across domains, so a new site needs only a small regressor.
  • Correlation filter.Conditional Closed-form, adding only K. Beneficial at n = 25, negligible at n = 113.
  • NNLS.Ablated The simplex constraint keeps predictions inside observed AGB. Benchmarked against ridge and XGBoost.
  • UMAP + K-means.Conditional Label-free, with a silhouette test deciding whether to apply it.

Operational logic

Each choice minimises cost and labelled data while staying robust at real inventory sizes.

Appendix A4 · Descriptors

The Descriptors Carry Signal

  • Span −0.843 to +0.871.
  • 171 of 384 significant at p < 0.05.
  • All top-10 exceed |r| = 0.814.
  • Selected K = 90 to 130, well below 384.

Why selection works at small n

Signal is spread widely; the highest-|r| dimensions concentrate it. The advantage fades as n grows.

Appendix A6 · Distribution

How Biomass Is Distributed

  • Balakot. 16 forested plots, 2.05 to 25.77 t ha⁻¹; 9 at zero.
  • Karlsruhe. 101 forest plots, 100.9 to 302.0 t ha⁻¹ across six species; 12 at zero.
  • Heterogeneity. Karlsruhe forms clearer groups, so one global model fits it less well.

Why this matters

The gap between the modes inflates label variance, and R² is computed against it.

Appendix A7 · Protocol

Implementation & Validation

Patches

  • Balakot: 152 px (about 35 m), cropped to 112, resized to 224.
  • Karlsruhe: 224 px (about 40 m) per plot, plus 12 non-forest patches.
  • ImageNet-normalised before extraction.

Validation

  • Balakot (n = 25): nested LOO. Everything fitted inside the training fold.
  • Karlsruhe (n = 113): 85/15 split within clusters, 5-fold inner CV tuning K and hyper-parameters.
  • Clustering fitted on training rows only.

Two caveats

The sites use different protocols, so their %RMSE are not comparable. The search budget is uneven: 84 NNLS configurations against 16 each for ridge and XGBoost.

Reproducibility

Public vit_small_patch16_224.dino weights as released; control ResNet50. All steps seeded. Only the regressor trains, so a run takes minutes.
