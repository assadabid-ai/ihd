Motivation & Problem

Why RGB-Only Biomass Estimation

  • Forests store about 861 Pg C and absorb a further 2.4–3.5 Pg C yr⁻¹. Above-ground biomass (AGB, t ha⁻¹) is the part of that stock inventories actually report, and the variable behind IPCC Tier-2/3 accounting, REDD+ and carbon credits.
  • Landscape-to-global AGB products still disagree by 20–50% where forests are heterogeneous or data-sparse. The sensors that would settle it are constrained: LiDAR is accurate but expensive, while multispectral and SAR saturate at high biomass.
  • Where the need is greatest, in Pakistan and comparable regions, LiDAR is effectively unavailable and field inventories for a district-scale study run to on the order of 100 plots.

Problem

There is no accurate, data-efficient, operationally affordable framework for plot-level forest AGB estimation and wall-to-wall carbon mapping that relies only on RGB imagery, requires no site-specific training of a deep feature extractor, and remains robust at the small sample sizes typical of national inventories in developing regions.

Driving Question

Can AGB be estimated accurately from the most widely available imagery: standard high-resolution RGB, captured with no specialised spectral hardware?

Research Questions & Objectives

One Central Question, Five Sub-Questions

Can a frozen, self-supervised ViT, applied as a fixed extractor to RGB aerial imagery, support accurate plot-level AGB regression across two distinct ecosystems, without site-specific fine-tuning?

Q1C1
Feature transfer. Do the DINO CLS descriptors encode enough canopy structure to recover AGB variance?
Q2C1
Small-sample regression. Which lightweight regressor (NNLS, ridge, or XGBoost) best converts descriptors to AGB when plots are scarce?
Q3C2
Dimensionality control. Does correlation-based descriptor selection beat the full 384-D vector?
Q4C3
Landscape heterogeneity. Does an unsupervised UMAP + K-means partition with cluster-specific regressors improve accuracy?
Q5C4
Multi-site consistency. Refitting only the regressor, does the unchanged pipeline give robust gains over the literature baselines at both sites?

The Four Contributions Tagged Above

C1 · Build the RGB-only AGB pipeline on DINO ViT-Small with lightweight regressors.  C2 · Regularise high-dimensional learned features by interpretable correlation-based selection.  C3 · Adapt to heterogeneous landscapes with optional unsupervised clustering, assessed by ablation.  C4 · Validate & map against the literature baselines at two distinct sites, and produce wall-to-wall carbon rasters.

Methodology

A Five-Stage RGB-Only Pipeline

  • 1 · Patch extraction. Crop a plot-centred RGB patch, resize to 224×224, and normalise with ImageNet statistics.
  • 2 · Descriptor extraction. Pass the patch through a frozen DINO ViT-Small to obtain a 384-D CLS-token descriptor. No fine-tuning, at either site.
  • 3 · Descriptor selection. Rank dimensions by |Pearson r| with AGB on the training set only, then keep the top K.
  • 4 · Unsupervised grouping. Embed with UMAP and cluster with K-means, fitting cluster-specific models where the data support it.
  • 5 · Regression. Apply NNLS, ridge, or XGBoost to predict plot-level AGB and wall-to-wall maps.

Key Principle

The encoder is never trained, so the same backbone serves both sites and only the lightweight regressor is fitted per site.

Methodology · Primary Regressor

Non-Negative Least Squares (NNLS)

An instance-based regressor. For a query patch it selects the k nearest training plots in descriptor space, reconstructs the query descriptor as a non-negative simplex combination of those neighbours, then applies the same weights to their measured AGB.

Step 1 · Reconstruct the query under a simplex constraint
minw ∈ ℝᵏ ‖ A w − b ‖₂²  subject to  w ≥ 0,  1ᵀw = 1
Step 2 · Predict AGB from the neighbour labels
ŷ = Σi=1k wᵢ yᵢ

A ∈ ℝd×k holds the k neighbour descriptors as columns, b ∈ ℝd is the query descriptor, w the weights, yi the AGB of neighbour i. Solved by the active-set algorithm (Lawson & Hanson 1974; Bro & De Jong 1997); k and the distance metric are chosen by cross-validation inside each training fold.

Lowest Error at Both Sites

DINO + NNLS leads at %RMSE = 24.71 (Balakot, R² = 0.922) and 17.40 (Karlsruhe, R² = 0.837), ahead of ridge and XGBoost at both sites.

Why NNLS Here

  • Small-sample robust. No global parametric fit, so it avoids the overfitting XGBoost shows at n = 25.
  • Bounded by construction. Non-negative weights, and under the simplex constraint a convex combination of observed AGB, so a prediction cannot go negative or run away past the training range.
  • Physically grounded. Non-negative weights match non-negative biomass, with no saturation at high AGB.
  • Interpretable. The weights are an explicit mixture over named reference plots.
  • Smooth maps. Convex blending yields spatially coherent wall-to-wall rasters.

Implementation

Implementation & Validation Protocol

Patches Per site

  • Pakistan: a 152×152 px patch (about 35 m) is centre-cropped to 112×112 and resized to 224×224 (bicubic).
  • Germany: a 224×224 px patch (about 40 m) is centred on each plot, plus 12 non-forest patches at 0 t ha⁻¹.
  • All patches are ImageNet-normalised before extraction.

Protocol Validation

  • Pakistan (n=25): nested LOO. Descriptor ranking, K, clustering and every hyper-parameter are fitted inside the training fold only, never on the held-out data. The same discipline applies to the literature baselines, so the comparison is like-for-like.
  • Germany (n=113): 85/15 split stratified within clusters (ntrain=96, ntest=17; clusters 37/59 train, 8/9 test), with 5-fold inner CV on the training rows tuning K and every regressor hyper-parameter.
  • K is swept over an 18-value grid from 10 to 360. Grids: NNLS 84 configs (neighbours k, cosine/Euclidean, simplex on/off, L2), ridge 16 (α 0.01–1000 × scaling), XGBoost 16. Metrics: %RMSE (primary), RMSE, MAE, R².

Reproducibility & Cost

Public vit_small_patch16_224.dino weights (Caron et al. 2021), 384-D CLS token, used as released; the control backbone is ImageNet-supervised ResNet50 (He et al. 2016). All stochastic steps are seeded (UMAP, K-means, K-fold random_state=42). Because only the small regressor trains, a full run finishes in minutes on a single mid-range GPU. Datasets are available to qualified researchers on request; an open code release is the immediate next step.

NNLS is the best regressor on DINO descriptors at both sites and the worst on ResNet50, despite ResNet50 carrying 5× the dimensions. No ResNet50 configuration beats the baseline at either site.

When Does Structure Actually Help?

The Diagnostic Decides Correctly at Both Sites

Karlsruhe has real structure, so it is partitioned: for NNLS specialisation is worth +4.2 pp and outweighs the 1.2 pp lost to smaller training sets. For XGBoost the damage is almost all data loss (4.4 of 5.1 pp): it is data-hungry, not harmed by structure. Balakot is a single cluster, so the pipeline keeps one global model; forcing a split there costs NNLS 24.71→64.62, which is exactly what the silhouette predicts.

Results · Wall-to-Wall Mapping

From Plots to Wall-to-Wall Maps

One Predictor Per Site, Applied Window by Window

Each scene is tiled into non-overlapping 395 px (Karlsruhe) and 152 px (Balakot) windows, encoded, and passed through that site's own fitted NNLS predictor: Karlsruhe uses its per-cluster CV-selected configuration, Balakot its reconstruction-adaptive one. Because every NNLS prediction is a non-negative mixture of measured plots, convex at Balakot, the maps stay inside the field-data range (0–25.4 and 0–253.8 t ha⁻¹) rather than extrapolating like ridge and XGBoost.

Why This Is the Deliverable

This is the output a forest department or a carbon-credit verifier can act on: a hectare-level carbon stock for a whole scene, derived from one ordinary RGB acquisition and a public vision model, with no LiDAR flight and no site-specific network training.

Read With Care

The conversion is a fixed scalar, so it adds no information and all map uncertainty is inherited from the AGB prediction. The 0.47 carbon fraction is itself a species-averaged default (IPCC 2006, Vol. 4, Ch. 4), not a locally calibrated value.

Limitations

What These Numbers Do Not Show

  • What the accuracy actually measures. The zero-biomass plots dominate the label variance, so R² mostly rewards telling forest from non-forest rather than resolving biomass within forest. %RMSE is robust to this, R² is not, and the within-forest signal is strong at Balakot but low at Karlsruhe.
  • Quality of the reference data. The models can only be as good as the field labels. Balakot's allometric model saturates, so nearly a third of its forested plots share a single value, and at Karlsruhe the image patch covers only the central part of the ground area each label refers to.
  • Generalisation is shown, not proven. Two sites, one acquisition each. The pipeline and the encoder are shared, but the regressor is always refitted, so nothing here demonstrates seasonal stability or that a model fitted at one site works at an unseen one.
  • The sensor sets a ceiling. RGB sees the canopy surface, not the vertical structure that carries most of the biomass. That limits the method wherever the canopy closes over and stands differ mainly in height, which is where LiDAR keeps its advantage.

Stated, Not Discovered

Every one of these was measured deliberately rather than found in review. Reporting where the method is weak is what makes the cases where it is strong credible.

Conclusions

Answers, Contributions & What Comes Next

A frozen, public vision encoder plus a convex instance-based regressor delivers plot-level AGB and carbon maps from ordinary RGB, and the advantage comes from the geometry of the embedding, not from depth or dimensionality.

The 97-Dimensional Baseline

The three families concatenate into a 97-dimensional vector, regressed with random forest, gradient boosting, XGBoost, ridge, stacking, and stepwise models, all tuned under the same protocol as the proposed pipeline. They are beaten by 20.6% (Balakot) and 23.6% (Karlsruhe) relative error.

Appendix A2 · Design Rationale

Why These Design Choices

  • Frozen DINO. Self-distillation yields embeddings stable across domains and resolutions, so deploying at a new site needs only a small regressor rather than encoder retraining.
  • Correlation filter. Closed-form and interpretable, it adds only K as a hyperparameter, requires no extra model fitting, and nests cleanly inside CV folds.
  • NNLS. An instance-based method whose simplex constraint (w ≥ 0, Σw = 1) makes each prediction a convex combination of observed AGB, so predictions never extrapolate beyond the training range.
  • UMAP + K-means. A label-free step that separates structurally distinct stands so each regressor specialises, falling back to one model when only a single group is supported.
  • Zero-biomass anchors. Pull non-forest areas (roads, fields) toward zero AGB at no extra data cost, suppressing artefacts in wall-to-wall maps.

Operational Logic

Every choice minimises cost and labelled-data demand while staying robust at the sample sizes real inventories in low-resource settings actually reach.

Appendix A4 · Descriptor Signal

The Descriptors Carry Real Biomass Signal

  • Descriptor–AGB correlations span −0.843 to +0.871 (mean r = −0.011, SD = 0.429).
  • 171 of 384 dimensions (44.5%) are significant at p < 0.05.
  • The top descriptor (feature 14) reaches r = 0.871 (p = 1.51×10⁻⁸), and all top-10 have |r| > 0.814.
  • The optimal window K = 90–130 sits well below 384, so low-ranked dimensions add noise on small data.

Why Selection Works

Biomass signal is spread broadly across the descriptor space, but the highest-|r| dimensions concentrate it through a closed-form, interpretable filter.

Appendix A6 · Data Distribution

How Biomass Is Distributed, and Why It Matters

  • Balakot 🇵🇰. 16 forested plots span 2.05–25.77 t ha⁻¹ (mean about 20.22), and 9 plots recorded zero AGB on naturally bare terrain, giving an overall range of 0–25.77.
  • Karlsruhe 🇩🇪. 101 forest plots span 100.9–302.0 t ha⁻¹ (mean about 175) across six species (Scots pine, European beech, sessile and red oak, wild cherry, hornbeam), plus 12 non-forest patches added at 0 t ha⁻¹.
  • Landscape heterogeneity. In Germany the descriptor space forms clear clusters with diverse biomass. Feature distance no longer tracks biomass difference, so a single global model struggles.

Design Implication

The contrasting distributions justify two choices: zero-biomass anchors for non-forest, and unsupervised clustering with per-cluster regressors for heterogeneous Germany.
